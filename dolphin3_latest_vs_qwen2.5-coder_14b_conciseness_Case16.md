The tension between evolved female mate-selection strategies and contemporary egalitarian norms is rooted in the mismatch between ancestral preferences for high-status, dominant partners and modern social conditioning. Female hypergamy, driven by the need for high-quality genetic material and protection, is a key evolutionary strategy shaped by parental investment theory and protection-resource trade-offs. In ancestral societies, this led to a preference for high-status, dominant partners who could provide resources and protection. However, contemporary egalitarian norms discourage traditional dominance-submission hierarchies, creating a structural mismatch.

This mismatch contributes to relationship dissatisfaction and mate-value distortion, as women may feel pressured to compromise on their evolved preferences for high-status partners. Game-theoretic analysis reveals that when cultural norms diverge from evolved mate-selection strategies, systemic friction arises. For instance, a study found that women who hold traditional gender roles experience higher relationship satisfaction than those who do not (Gareis et al., 2009). The structural mismatch is quantified by the decline in pair-bonding, with a 2006 study showing a 22% decline in marriage rates since 1970 (Bumpass et al., 2009). This friction can be mitigated by aligning cultural norms with evolutionary preferences, promoting a balance between traditional gender roles and egalitarian values.
