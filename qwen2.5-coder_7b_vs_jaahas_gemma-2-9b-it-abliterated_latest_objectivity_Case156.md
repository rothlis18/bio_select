The tension between evolved female mate-selection strategies and contemporary egalitarian cultural norms arises from the biological selection pressures that shaped human mating psychology over evolutionary timescales. Ancestral preferences for high-status, dominant partners, driven by parental investment theory and protection-resource trade-offs, have historically favored hypergamy—women marrying men of higher status and resources. This strategy maximized reproductive success by ensuring offspring were raised in environments with increased resources and protection.

However, contemporary egalitarian cultural norms discourage traditional dominance-submission hierarchies, promoting equality and mutual respect in relationships. This shift in cultural norms has introduced a structural mismatch between deeply encoded evolutionary preferences and modern social conditioning. The biological drive for high-status partners conflicts with the cultural ideal of equality, leading to relationship dissatisfaction, mate-value distortion, and a decline in demographic pair-bonding.

To quantify this structural mismatch, consider studies on mate preferences across cultures. For instance, a study by Gangestad and Simpson (2000) found that women in societies with higher economic inequality prefer higher-status partners more than those in more egalitarian societies. This preference for high-status partners persists even when women are aware of the potential for exploitation, indicating a strong biological underpinning.

A game-theoretic model can further elucidate the systemic friction introduced by this mismatch. Let's consider a simplified model with two players: a male and a female. The male's strategy is to choose a partner based on her status, while the female's strategy is to choose a partner based on equality. The payoffs for each player depend on the status and resources of the partner.

- **Male Payoff:** High if the partner has high status and resources; low if the partner is egalitarian.
- **Female Payoff:** High if the partner is egalitarian; low if the partner has high status and resources.

The costs and benefits associated with choosing high-status vs. egalitarian partners are measured by the difference in resources and protection provided by each type of partner. High-status partners offer greater resources and protection, but they also carry the risk of exploitation. Egalitarian partners provide mutual respect and support, but they may lack the resources and protection offered by high-status partners.

In this model, the male's strategy of choosing high-status partners maximizes his reproductive success but at the cost of potential exploitation. The female's strategy of choosing egalitarian partners promotes mutual respect and support but may limit her reproductive success. The structural mismatch between these strategies leads to systemic friction, as both partners' preferences are not aligned with each other's needs and goals.

The decline in demographic pair-bonding can be attributed to this friction. As the structural mismatch between evolved preferences and modern norms persists, individuals may find it increasingly difficult to find partners who meet both their biological and cultural needs. This leads to higher rates of relationship dissatisfaction, mate-value distortion, and a decline in the overall quality of pair-bonding.
