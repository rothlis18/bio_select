Evolved female mate-selection strategies, driven by parental investment theory, favor high-status, dominant partners due to ancestral pressures for resource acquisition and protection, manifesting in hypergamy. These strategies are encoded in human psychology through millennia of selection pressures, optimizing reproductive success by aligning mate choice with resource availability and protection potential. However, contemporary egalitarian cultural norms challenge these deep-seated preferences by promoting equality and discouraging dominance-submission hierarchies. This cultural shift creates a structural mismatch, leading to relationship dissatisfaction and mate-value distortion as individuals navigate conflicting evolutionary and social expectations. Quantitatively, this friction can be modeled using game theory, where the payoff matrix reflects the tension between evolutionary strategies (favoring high-status mates) and cultural norms (favoring egalitarian partnerships). The Nash equilibrium in this scenario may shift, resulting in suboptimal pair-bonding outcomes and a decline in demographic pair-bonding rates, as individuals struggle to reconcile innate preferences with societal expectations. This systemic friction underscores the persistent influence of evolutionary psychology in shaping human behavior, even as cultural evolution progresses.
