The tension between evolved female mate-selection strategies and contemporary egalitarian cultural norms stems from biological selection pressures that shaped human mating psychology over millions of years. Evolutionary theories, such as parental investment theory and protection-resource trade-offs, favor high-status, dominant partners. This preference for dominance is evident in hypergamy, where women often seek partners with higher resources and status. However, contemporary egalitarian cultures discourage traditional dominance-submission hierarchies, leading to a structural mismatch. This mismatch contributes to relationship dissatisfaction, mate-value distortion, and a decline in demographic pair-bonding. A game-theoretic analysis reveals that the systemic friction introduced by divergent cultural norms can lead to suboptimal mating outcomes. For instance, the preference for high-status partners may conflict with the egalitarian ideal of mutual respect and equality, resulting in mate selection that does not maximize overall reproductive success. This friction can manifest as increased conflict, reduced mating success, and a shift towards less stable relationships. Therefore, understanding and addressing this mismatch is crucial for improving relationship satisfaction and demographic health.
