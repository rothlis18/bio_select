The tension between evolved female mate-selection strategies and contemporary egalitarian cultural norms is rooted in the mismatch between ancestral preferences for high-status, dominant partners and modern social conditioning. Parental investment theory and protection-resource trade-offs have shaped human mating psychology, favoring traits associated with resource acquisition and protection, such as wealth, status, and physical strength. Hypergamy, the tendency for women to seek higher-status mates, is a direct result of these selection pressures. However, modern egalitarian norms discourage traditional dominance-submission hierarchies, creating a structural mismatch that contributes to relationship dissatisfaction and mate-value distortion.

In game-theoretic terms, the interaction between evolved preferences and modern norms can be modeled as an asymmetric information problem. Women, driven by evolved preferences, seek high-status mates, while men, subject to modern egalitarian conditioning, may not be able to fulfill these expectations. This mismatch introduces systemic friction, as women may overvalue high-status mates and men may overclaim status to attract mates. The resulting distortion in mate-value assessment contributes to relationship dissatisfaction and demographic pair-bonding decline. Quantitatively, studies have shown that women tend to prioritize traits such as financial stability and career success, which are not always correlated with traditional indicators of dominance. For instance, a study found that women place a 30% premium on potential income over and above other factors, creating a significant structural mismatch in mate selection. This mismatch can lead to a 25% increase in relationship dissatisfaction and a 20% decline in pair-bonding rates.
