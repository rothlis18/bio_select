**Analysis:**

1. **Ancestral Female Mate-Selection Strategies:**
   - Parental investment theory posits that females prefer mates who can provide resources and protection, as they invest more in offspring (Trivers, 1972).
   - Dominance hierarchies were crucial for accessing resources and protection, leading to evolved preferences for high-status, dominant partners (Buss, 1989).
   - Hypergamy, the preference for mates above one's own social standing, evolved as a strategy to maximize offspring quality and quantity (Buss & Schmitt, 1993).

2. **Contemporary Egalitarian Cultural Norms:**
   - Modern cultures emphasize egalitarianism, discouraging traditional dominance-submission hierarchies and promoting gender equality (Lerner, 1986).
   - These norms encourage mutual respect, cooperation, and shared decision-making in relationships.

3. **Structural Mismatch and Its Consequences:**
   - The mismatch between evolved preferences and modern social conditioning introduces systemic friction, leading to relationship dissatisfaction and mate-value distortion.
   - Women may experience cognitive dissonance when their egalitarian values clash with their evolved preferences for dominant partners (Norton & Gino, 2014).
   - Men may struggle with the expectation to be egalitarian while also fulfilling evolved male roles, such as providing and protecting.

4. **Game-Theoretic Analysis:**
   - Consider a simple game where players can choose between traditional (T) and egalitarian (E) mating strategies. Let's assume:
     - Traditional strategy payoff (P_T) = 5 (high resource provisioning, protection)
     - Egalitarian strategy payoff (P_E) = 3 (mutual respect, cooperation)
     - Mixed strategy payoff (P_M) = 4 (compromise between T and E)
   - If both players choose T, P_T = 5. If both choose E, P_E = 3. If one chooses T and the other E, P_M = 4.
   - Evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS) analysis shows that the mixed strategy (both players choosing E) is the Nash equilibrium, as deviating from it reduces payoff (Maynard Smith, 1982).
   - However, this equilibrium may not be stable due to the structural mismatch, leading to suboptimal outcomes and relationship dissatisfaction.

5. **Demographic Pair-Bonding Decline:**
   - The mismatch may contribute to demographic pair-bonding decline by increasing relationship instability and decreasing fertility rates (e.g., through delayed marriage and childbearing).
   - Cultural norms that diverge from evolved preferences may lead to lower mate-value satisfaction, reducing commitment and investment in long-term pair-bonds.

**Quantification:**
- Let's define mate-value satisfaction (MVS) as the difference between evolved preferences and cultural norms: MVS = |P_T - P_E|.
- In the game-theoretic analysis, MVS = |5 - 3| = 2 when both players choose E, indicating a structural mismatch.
- To quantify the demographic impact, consider the fertility rate difference (ΔFR) between the traditional and egalitarian strategies: ΔFR = FR_T - FR_E.
- If FR_T = 2.5 and FR_E = 2.0, then ΔFR = 0.5, suggesting a potential decline in fertility rates due to the structural mismatch.

**Conclusion:**
The tension between evolved female mate-selection strategies and contemporary egalitarian cultural norms introduces systemic friction, leading to relationship dissatisfaction, mate-value distortion, and potential demographic pair-bonding decline. Game-theoretic analysis suggests that the mixed strategy of egalitarian norms is evolutionarily stable but may not be optimal or stable due to the structural mismatch. Quantifying these effects can help inform policy and cultural interventions aimed at mitigating the negative consequences of this mismatch.
