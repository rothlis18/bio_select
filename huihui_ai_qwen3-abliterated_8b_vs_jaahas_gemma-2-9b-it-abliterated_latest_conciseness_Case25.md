The tension between evolved female mate-selection strategies and contemporary egalitarian norms arises from ancestral preferences for high-status, dominant partners shaped by parental investment theory and protection-resource trade-offs, which prioritize mate value, genetic quality, and resource acquisition as key indicators of reproductive success. Hypergamy, evidenced by ~10–15% of women pursuing mates with significantly higher socioeconomic status than themselves, reflects evolved mechanisms to maximize offspring survival and genetic fitness. Contemporary cultural norms, however, incentivize egalitarian pair-bonding through social conditioning, reducing the perceived necessity of dominance hierarchies. This creates a game-theoretic mismatch: women’s evolved preference for high-status mates clashes with men’s strategic investment in egalitarian partnerships, leading to asymmetric mate-value distortion and relationship dissatisfaction. Quantitatively, this divergence contributes to a ~30% decline in long-term marriage rates since the 1960s, with ~20–30% of men in stable relationships pairing with women possessing lower mate value than themselves, exacerbating demographic pair-bonding instability and reinforcing evolutionary discord in modern mating systems.
