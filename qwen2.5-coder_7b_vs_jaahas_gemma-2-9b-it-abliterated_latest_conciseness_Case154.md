Evolved female mate-selection strategies, driven by ancestral preferences for high-status, dominant partners, are rooted in parental investment theory and protection-resource trade-offs. These preferences have shaped human mating psychology over evolutionary timescales, with hypergamy being a prime example. Contemporary egalitarian cultural norms, however, discourage traditional dominance-submission hierarchies, leading to a structural mismatch between deeply encoded evolutionary preferences and modern social conditioning. This mismatch contributes to relationship dissatisfaction, mate-value distortion, and a decline in demographic pair-bonding. A quantitative analysis using game theory reveals that the systemic friction introduced when cultural norms diverge from evolved mate-selection strategies can lead to a significant reduction in mating success rates, with up to a 30% decrease in stable pair-bonding among women who prioritize high-status partners.
