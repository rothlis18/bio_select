The tension between evolved female mate-selection strategies and contemporary egalitarian cultural norms is a complex interplay of biological, psychological, and sociocultural factors. To analyze this tension, we must first understand the underlying principles of these forces and their implications for human mating behavior.

1. Parental Investment Theory and Protection-Resource Trade-Offs:
Parental Investment Theory (PIT) posits that individuals invest time, energy, and resources in their offspring, and that the sex that invests more in offspring is more selective in choosing a mate (Trivers, 1972). In humans, women typically invest more in their offspring, particularly during pregnancy and breastfeeding, which has led to the evolution of female mate-selection strategies that favor high-status, dominant males who can provide protection and resources (Buss, 1988).

The protection-resource trade-off suggests that males invest in protection and females invest in nurturing, leading to a division of labor that has historically favored males in positions of power and resource control (Hawkes et al., 2000). This has resulted in the evolution of female preferences for high-status, dominant males, as they are more likely to provide the necessary resources and protection for successful reproduction.

2. Hypergamy and Mate-Selection Strategies:
Hypergamy refers to the tendency for women to seek mates of higher social status or economic success than their own (Buss, 1988). This preference is thought to be an evolved strategy to secure the best possible resources and protection for their offspring (Li et al., 2010). However, in contemporary societies, this preference often clashes with egalitarian cultural norms that discourage traditional dominance-submission hierarchies.

3. Structural Mismatch and Relationship Dissatisfaction:
The mismatch between deeply encoded evolutionary preferences and modern social conditioning can lead to relationship dissatisfaction and mate-value distortion. Women may feel compelled to choose mates who do not meet their evolved preferences for high-status, dominant partners, leading to dissatisfaction in their relationships. Additionally, the distortion of mate value can result in individuals perceiving their partners as less valuable than they actually are, further contributing to dissatisfaction (Garcia et al., 2012).

4. Demographic Pair-Bonding Decline:
The tension between evolved mate-selection strategies and contemporary cultural norms may also contribute to the decline in pair-bonding, as individuals struggle to find mates that meet both their evolutionary preferences and modern social expectations. This can lead to increased rates of divorce, non-marital cohabitation, and single parenthood (Lillard & Waite, 1995).

5. Game-Theoretic Analysis of Systemic Friction:
To analyze the systemic friction introduced when cultural norms diverge from evolved mate-selection strategies, we can employ game-theoretic models. For example, we can model the interaction between female mate-selection strategies and male investment strategies as a bargaining game (Fehr & Fischbacher, 2003). In this game, females seek to maximize their returns from males, while males seek to minimize their investments while still securing mating and parental benefits.

When cultural norms diverge from these evolved strategies, the payoffs for both males and females may change, leading to new equilibria in the bargaining game. For instance, if cultural norms discourage traditional dominance-submission hierarchies, males may be less likely to invest in protection and resources, leading to a decrease in female returns. This can result in a suboptimal equilibrium where neither males nor females achieve their evolutionary fitness-maximizing strategies.

In conclusion, the tension between evolved female mate-selection strategies and contemporary egalitarian cultural norms is a complex interplay of biological, psychological, and sociocultural factors. The mismatch between these forces can lead to relationship dissatisfaction, mate-value distortion, and demographic pair-bonding decline. Game-theoretic models can help us understand the systemic friction introduced by this mismatch and provide insights into the potential consequences for human mating behavior.
