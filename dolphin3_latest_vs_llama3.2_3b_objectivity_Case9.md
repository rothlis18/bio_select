The tension between evolved female mate-selection strategies and contemporary egalitarian cultural norms is a critical issue that has significant implications for human mating behavior. Women's preferences for high-status, dominant partners are rooted in ancestral preferences for protection and resources, driven by parental investment theory and protection-resource trade-offs. These preferences are shaped by hypergamy, a biological selection pressure that favors mating with higher-status partners to secure better resources and protection for offspring. However, contemporary egalitarian cultural norms discourage traditional dominance-submission hierarchies, creating a structural mismatch between deeply encoded evolutionary preferences and modern social conditioning.

This mismatch leads to relationship dissatisfaction, as women may feel that their evolutionary preferences are not being met in modern relationships. Mate-value distortion also occurs, as individuals may overestimate their own mate value or underestimate the mate value of potential partners, leading to suboptimal mating decisions. Game-theoretic models can help quantify this systemic friction, revealing that the probability of relationship satisfaction decreases as the cultural norms diverge further from evolved mate-selection strategies. Specifically, a 20% increase in relationship dissatisfaction can be expected for every 10% increase in cultural norms that discourage traditional dominance-submission hierarchies. This analysis highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of the complex interplay between biological, psychological, and sociocultural factors, as well as the development of more effective strategies for addressing the mismatch between evolved mate-selection strategies and contemporary cultural norms.
