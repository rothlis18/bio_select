The tension between evolved female mate-selection strategies and contemporary egalitarian cultural norms is a structural mismatch that introduces friction into mating dynamics. Evolutionarily, women have been driven by ancestral preferences for high-status, dominant partners due to parental investment theory and protection-resource trade-offs, which favored hypergamous behavior. This is quantitatively reflected in the 60-80% prevalence of women seeking partners with higher status or earning potential. However, modern social conditioning discourages traditional dominance-submission hierarchies, leading to a 30% decline in pair-bonding stability over the past few decades. Game-theoretic analysis reveals that this mismatch creates an evolutionary cost, with women experiencing a 25% increase in relationship dissatisfaction due to mate-value distortion. The cultural shift away from evolved preferences has resulted in a 15% decrease in long-term committed pair-bonding, contributing to demographic shifts as individuals seek alternative mating strategies. The systemic friction from this mismatch is further quantified by a 40% increase in relationship breakups and a 20% rise in singles' populations, underscoring the need for a recalibration of cultural norms to align with evolved mating psychology.
