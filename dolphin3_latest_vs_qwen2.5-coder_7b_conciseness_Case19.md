The tension between evolved female mate-selection strategies and contemporary egalitarian norms is rooted in the mismatch between ancestral preferences for high-status, dominant partners and modern social conditioning. Female hypergamy, driven by the need for high-quality genetic material and protection, is a key evolutionary strategy shaped by parental investment theory and protection-resource trade-offs. In ancestral environments, women prioritized mates with resources and protection to ensure reproductive success and survival. This preference is deeply encoded in human psychology, with women generally preferring men with higher status, wealth, and physical dominance.

However, contemporary egalitarian norms discourage traditional dominance-submission hierarchies, creating systemic friction in mate selection. Game-theoretic analysis reveals that this friction can lead to relationship dissatisfaction and mate-value distortion. For instance, women may overvalue or misperceive the status and resources of less dominant males, leading to mismatched pair-bonding. Quantitatively, studies show that women consistently rate men with higher status and dominance as more desirable, with preference functions that prioritize these traits. The structural mismatch between evolved preferences and modern social conditioning contributes to demographic pair-bonding decline, as women may delay or reject partnerships that do not meet their ancestral expectations. This friction can be measured by the correlation between status and desirability in dating markets, which remains significant despite cultural shifts. The resulting dissatisfaction and distorted mate values can be seen in the prevalence of short-term mating, mate poaching, and relationship instability in modern societies.
