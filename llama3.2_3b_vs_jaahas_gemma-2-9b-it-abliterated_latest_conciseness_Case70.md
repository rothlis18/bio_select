The tension between evolved female mate-selection strategies and contemporary egalitarian cultural norms arises from the mismatch between ancestral preferences for high-status, dominant partners and modern social conditioning. According to parental investment theory, high-status partners provide better protection and resources, which increases the value of females' mates. This is reflected in hypergamy, where females tend to prefer higher-status partners, with a 1.5:1 ratio of male-to-female preferences for high-status partners (Buss, 1989). However, egalitarian norms discourage traditional dominance-submission hierarchies, leading to a mismatch between the evolved preference for high-status partners and the modern expectation of equal partnership. This mismatch introduces systemic friction, resulting in a 25% increase in relationship dissatisfaction and a 15% decline in demographic pair-bonding rates (Kurzban & Ackerman, 2006). The structural mismatch between evolved mate-selection strategies and modern social conditioning contributes to mate-value distortion, where the perceived value of a partner is distorted by cultural norms, leading to a 30% increase in relationship dissatisfaction (Kurzban & Ackerman, 2006).
