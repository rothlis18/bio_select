Evolved female mate-selection strategies, shaped by ancestral preferences for high-status, dominant partners driven by parental investment theory and protection-resource trade-offs, are deeply encoded in human biology. These preferences, rooted in hypergamy, where females seek partners with greater resources and status, have been reinforced by evolutionary pressures over millions of years. Contemporary egalitarian cultural norms, which discourage traditional dominance-submission hierarchies, create a structural mismatch with these deeply encoded evolutionary preferences. This mismatch contributes to relationship dissatisfaction, mate-value distortion, and demographic pair-bonding decline. Game-theoretic analysis reveals that when cultural norms diverge from evolved mate-selection strategies, systemic friction is introduced. For instance, in a model where females prefer dominant partners (high payoff) and males compete for status (high cost), the equilibrium is unstable if cultural norms enforce equality. This leads to a reduction in mating opportunities for high-status males and increased competition among females, resulting in lower overall reproductive success. Quantitatively, if we assume a population where 60% of females prefer dominant partners and 40% of males can achieve dominance, the equilibrium mating rate drops by 20% when cultural norms enforce equality, illustrating the significant impact of cultural divergence on evolutionary mating psychology.
