The tension between evolved female mate-selection strategies and contemporary egalitarian cultural norms arises from the biological selection pressures that shaped human mating psychology over evolutionary timescales. Ancestral preferences for high-status, dominant partners, driven by parental investment theory and protection-resource trade-offs, have been reinforced through mechanisms such as hypergamy. Hypergamy, where females preferentially mate with higher-status males, has historically ensured genetic quality and resource allocation. However, contemporary egalitarian cultural norms discourage traditional dominance-submission hierarchies, leading to a structural mismatch between deeply encoded evolutionary preferences and modern social conditioning. This mismatch contributes to relationship dissatisfaction, mate-value distortion, and demographic pair-bonding decline. A quantitative analysis using game theory reveals that the systemic friction introduced when cultural norms diverge from evolved mate-selection strategies can be modeled as a deviation from the Nash equilibrium in a mating market. Specifically, the introduction of egalitarian norms can lead to a shift in the optimal mating strategy for females, resulting in a lower overall mating success rate and increased conflict within relationships. The game-theoretic model predicts that the equilibrium mating strategy under egalitarian norms will be less favorable for females compared to the historical equilibrium under hypergamy, thereby exacerbating the tension between evolved preferences and modern cultural norms.
