The tension between evolved female mate-selection strategies and contemporary egalitarian cultural norms arises from the biological selection pressures that shaped human mating psychology over evolutionary timescales. Ancestral preferences for high-status, dominant partners, driven by parental investment theory and protection-resource trade-offs, have been reinforced through mechanisms such as hypergamy. This preference for higher-status partners can lead to mate-value distortion, where individuals may overvalue traits associated with status and undervalue traits associated with equality. The structural mismatch between deeply encoded evolutionary preferences and modern social conditioning contributes to relationship dissatisfaction and demographic pair-bonding decline. A quantitative analysis using game theory reveals that when cultural norms diverge from evolved mate-selection strategies, the strategic payoff matrices shift, leading to suboptimal outcomes for both individuals and the population as a whole. For instance, if the payoff for choosing a partner based on status is higher than on equality, individuals may be incentivized to prioritize status, resulting in a higher prevalence of high-status relationships and a decline in egalitarian pair-bonding. This systemic friction can be quantified by modeling the payoffs associated with different mating strategies and analyzing the resulting evolutionary dynamics.
